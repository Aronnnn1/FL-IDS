Boosting vs. Deep Learning, and the full FL-IDS pipeline

A federated, cascade-based intrusion detection system for IoT/IIoT traffic: a supervised LightGBM classifier as the first pass, with a client-side autoencoder as a federated, poisoning-resistant backstop for zero-day attacks. This site reports (1) a head-to-head comparison of boosting against a deep-learning classifier, (2) how long things take and why, (3) how the classifier was pushed past 0.95 macro F1 and 95% accuracy — and checked on a stricter, connection-held-out test, (4) a visual and step-by-step explanation of the whole pipeline, and (5) verification that it works end to end, with its limitations stated plainly. Every table of numbers is collected on the cumulative results page.

Dataset: Edge-IIoTset (Ferrag et al., 2022). All numbers come from real runs of the code in this repository. Full notes and sources: PAPER_NOTES.md.

Project status: complete (16 October 2026). Every phase is implemented, run on the real data and verified end to end; the research paper is being written.

What is new in FL-IDS

Each part of FL-IDS on its own has published precedent (listed below). What we did not find in the literature is all five working together as one system, plus the four contributions of our own listed here. Every claim points to a measured result on this site.

Boosting vs. a supervised DL classifier

If you swap LightGBM boosting for a deep-learning classifier as the cascade's first pass — same attack-type labels, same task, comparable effort — which is more accurate? Both were trained and evaluated on the exact same 500,000-row sample (400k train / 100k test, stratified); boosting on raw features, the DL classifier on standardized features (each model gets the input representation it needs).

Boosting (LightGBM)DL classifier (dense MLP, 8,271 params)

Per-class recall — where the gap actually is

Boosting doesn't win uniformly. The gap concentrates on application-layer attacks that look like legitimate traffic; on the flood attacks both models are essentially perfect.

Why boosting wins here. Gradient-boosted trees build axis-aligned decision splits directly on the engineered flow features (packet counts, flags, protocol fields) — exactly the thresholded, tabular structure this data has. A small dense network has to learn the same decision surface through weighted sums and nonlinearities, with far less inductive bias toward tabular data, and with 400k rows and 15 epochs it doesn't get there for classes whose boundary with “Normal” is subtle (XSS, SQL_injection, Uploading, DDoS_HTTP). On the flood attacks, where a couple of features separate the classes almost perfectly, both models hit ~100%.
Honest caveat. This DL classifier was deliberately modest — 2 hidden layers, 8,271 parameters, 15 epochs — a comparable-effort baseline, not an exhaustively tuned one. A larger or longer-trained network might close some of the gap; that wasn't tested. The right claim is “this DL classifier doesn't beat boosting here,” not “DL cannot work on this problem.”

Boosting

Boosting's errors sit mostly inside one application-layer cluster — DDoS_HTTP, Password, SQL_injection, Uploading, XSS and Normal — which together account for … of all its mistakes. Flood attacks (DDoS_TCP/UDP/ICMP) are almost never confused with anything.

DL classifier

The DL classifier's confusion is much more spread out and dominated by one column: Password absorbs large fractions of DDoS_HTTP, Normal, SQL_injection, Uploading and Vulnerability_scanner — a strong “when unsure, guess Password” bias boosting doesn't show, and the concrete mechanism behind its much higher benign false-positive rate (real Normal traffic pulled toward Password). The same cluster holds … of its mistakes, but it also fails on Port_Scanning ↔ DDoS_TCP and Fingerprinting, which boosting handles.

Final classifier (after the improvements below)

Boosting with the new service-port, content and flow features and class weights, on all 1,985,768 rows: trained on 1,429,825 and tested on 397,073 rows from whole connections it never saw during training (flow-grouped 80/20 split, seed 42). The application-layer cluster that held most of the errors above is now almost clean: the HTTP-family cluster holds … of the remaining mistakes. What is left is mostly Password ↔ DDoS_HTTP (both are HTTP requests to the same web server) and Ransomware → Backdoor (same port). MITM has no row here: it is a single ARP connection, so it lands wholly on one side of a connection-level split (on the random split its F1 is …).

Training time — how long, and why

Short answer: training either classifier is quick (boosting ~1 minute, DL ~10 seconds). What is slow is the evaluation methodology — the poisoning sweep and zero-day experiments retrain the whole federated pipeline 75 and 14 times — and within each of those runs, one component dominates.

Why a full federated run takes ~4 minutes when a round takes seconds

  • Boosting updates are the bottleneck. In the sweep logs a normal round takes ~5–7 s, but a round that includes a boosting update takes ~43 s: clients spend ~10 s filtering their traffic through a now-larger model, then the server spends ~33 s continuing LightGBM training (+50 trees × 15 classes) on the calibration set plus alerts. With an update every 3 rounds there are 6 per run, which by the log timestamps accounts for roughly three-quarters of a run's time (an estimate, not a profiler measurement).
  • Deterministic, single-threaded LightGBM, on purpose. Multi-threaded histogram building is not bit-reproducible: with identical inputs and seed one class's recall swung between 0.0 and 0.88 across runs (PAPER_NOTES §6). The code forces deterministic=True, force_row_wise and num_threads=1, trading speed on a 4-vCPU VM for reproducible science.
  • The model grows during training. Each update adds 50 trees per class (300 → 600 trees over a run), so every later round's client-side filtering and every later update is slower than the last.
  • The evaluation multiplies everything. Every sweep point, ablation variant and held-out class is a complete retraining from scratch (75 + 14 + the 5-variant ablation), so minutes per run become hours.
  • CPU-only, virtualised, memory-constrained hardware. No GPU (irrelevant here — the networks are tiny), 4 vCPUs, and a real multi-process run is memory-bound: 11 Python processes each importing PyTorch/LightGBM nearly exhausted the VM until thread pools were capped.
  • Data loading is paid on every script start. Parsing and cleaning the 1.16 GB CSV takes 15–25 s each time.
How to speed it up (none applied yet): cache the cleaned matrix as Parquet/NPZ instead of re-parsing the CSV; update boosting every 6 rounds or add fewer trees per update; allow multi-threaded LightGBM for exploratory runs (keeping the deterministic setting for reported numbers); and run evaluation sweeps on a machine with enough RAM to run two in parallel.

How macro F1 was pushed past 0.95

The target was ≥ 95% accuracy and ≥ 0.95 macro F1. With the original 91 features, boosting reached 0.887 macro F1 on the 500k comparison sample, and 0.889 macro F1 / 96.4% accuracy on all 1.9M rows. Ideas 1, 2, 7 and 8 below were implemented and measured; together they bring the classifier to 0.983 macro F1 and 99.7% accuracy on connections held out of training. Ideas 3–6 turned out not to be needed and were not run.

Before: the F1 budget of the original features

Macro F1 is the plain average of 15 per-class F1 scores, so reaching 0.95 needed the per-class F1 sum to rise from … to 14.25 — a gain of … F1 points spread over the weak classes. Bars show each class's F1 on the 500k comparison; the red part is its shortfall to 0.95.

Why the original features were stuck

In SQL_injection, XSS, Uploading and Password traffic only about 5–17% of packets carry any HTTP content. The rest are bare TCP handshake/acknowledgement packets to port 80 on the same web server, and those look identical whichever attack produced them. Measured directly: 2.31% of rows share an identical 91-feature vector with a row of a different class, which caps macro recall at 0.969 for any per-packet model. The information that separates these attacks was in the columns the dataset recipe drops.

After: each change, measured

LightGBM on all rows, 80/20. “Random” splits individual packets; “flow-grouped” keeps every connection entirely in training or entirely in test (the stricter number).

Original 91 featuresFinal (content + flow + class weights)

done1. Move from per-packet to behaviour features

What was done: packets are grouped into connections (IP pair, protocol, port pair, in capture order; a new connection after 60 s idle or 120 s active — CICFlowMeter's default active timeout). Every packet gets 20 connection-level features: packet count, duration, rate, SYN/FIN/RST counts, payload totals, HTTP request count and attack-keyword totals. Content-free ACK packets now carry what their connection did.

Result: the single biggest jump — flow-grouped macro F1 0.923 → 0.981. IP addresses and times are used only to group packets, never as features.

done2. Restore content signals the recipe drops — as numbers, not text

What was done: 18 numeric features from tcp.payload, http.request.full_uri and http.file_data: payload length, byte entropy, printable share, HTTP request flag and status code, header counts, URI length/parameters/%-encoding, special-character count, and counts of six generic signature groups (SQL keywords, script/XSS, upload, login/password, path traversal/commands, scanner tool names). Plus one service-port feature: the server side of the connection (80, 1883, 4321…); ephemeral client ports are excluded because their ranges follow capture time.

Result: the service port alone lifts flow-grouped macro F1 0.889 → 0.924; content on its own adds little (only a few packets carry it) but feeds the flow features above.

not needed3. Per-class decision thresholds instead of plain argmax

Not run: after 1, 2 and 7 the precision/recall asymmetry it targeted is gone (e.g. Uploading precision/recall now ~1.00/1.00).

not needed4. A specialist second-level model for the confusable cluster

Not run: the confusable cluster that held … of the original errors is now almost clean (see the final confusion matrix). The remaining Password ↔ DDoS_HTTP mix-up is ~2–4%.

partly5. Tune hyperparameters, with early stopping

Only lightly: 63 leaves, learning rate 0.1, feature/bagging fraction 0.8, up to 2,000 rounds with early stopping (patience 50) on a 10% validation split, leaf regularisation kept. No search was run; the features did the work.

not needed6. Other boosters, or an ensemble

Not run. LightGBM alone already sits close to the ambiguity ceiling of the new feature set (0.9999 accuracy).

done7. Class weights for rare classes

What was done: square-root inverse-frequency sample weights (a rare class counts more, without the instability of full inverse-frequency weights). Result: without weights, flow-grouped macro F1 swung between seeds (0.981 / 0.956 / 0.952) because Fingerprinting (156 connections) scored 0.49–0.88; with weights it is stable at 0.983–0.985 and Fingerprinting ~0.93. Even trained on only the 5% server calibration set, the classifier reaches 99.7% / 0.979.

done8. Check the target is real before chasing it

What was done: every result is reported on a flow-grouped split (stratified group k-fold on the connection id), so no connection is on both sides, and repeated over seeds 42/43/44. The first attempt exposed a real problem: the only benign VNC session is one 264,094-packet connection; without an active timeout it landed wholly in test and 16% of Normal was mislabelled. The 120 s active timeout (the standard flow-meter setting) fixed that. The original 91 features score about the same on both splits (0.887 vs 0.889), so they were not inflated by connection memorisation either.

Assumptions behind the final number

  • Classification is per connection record (up to 120 s): flow features summarise the whole record, so this is not a first-packet decision.
  • The service port partly benefits from the testbed: benign traffic here is MQTT (1883) and VNC (5900), never HTTP.
  • Natural class distribution (71% benign), all rows after de-duplication, 3 seeds.
  • The same features now run in the whole federated pipeline and in Phase B; their system-level results are under Verification.

The pipeline at a glance

Two phases, kept deliberately separate: Phase A trains the models offline (federated), and Phase B loads the saved bundle and enforces decisions on live traffic.

Phase A — data to trained bundle

Data Boosting (supervised) Federated clients / autoencoder Server-side robustness Network / SDN Output artefact

Phase B — traffic to enforcement

The cascade decision rule (one implementation, used by evaluation, dashboard and SDN)

  1. Boosting predicts a class and its probability.
  2. If the class is a known attack and probability ≥ 0.7 → final label is that attack type. The autoencoder is not consulted.
  3. Otherwise (predicted Normal, or an attack without enough confidence) the row goes to the autoencoder: reconstruction error > the client's threshold → “anomalous” (a zero-day signal), confidence = min(1, error/threshold − 1); else benign.

Every step explained

What each stage does, how it works, and the parameters that control it. Values are the ones used in this project's runs (configs/config.yaml); module paths are relative to fl_ids/.

1Load, clean and encode the datadata/pipeline.py · data/repair.py

Function

Turn the authors' CSV into a clean numeric matrix X (1,909,719 rows × 91 features) and integer labels y (15 classes).

How it works

  • Read 47 of 63 columns: 15 ID/timestamp/payload columns are dropped per the dataset authors' own recipe, plus Attack_label (derivable from the target, so keeping it would leak the answer).
  • Canonicalise the placeholder. “Field doesn't apply” is written 0 in some captures and 0.0 in others, and the spelling follows the label — so mqtt.topic_0.0 alone separated benign from attack at AUROC 1.0. Both spellings become 0; the strongest single feature is now ~0.71.
  • Drop NaN and duplicate rows (309,497 duplicates).
  • Repair MITM. The authors' MITM CSV has values under the wrong column names; its 1,214 rows are replaced by 1,229 rows re-extracted from their own pcap with tshark.
  • One-hot encode 7 categorical columns → 91 features.

2Service-port, content and flow features (classifier accuracy)data/features.py · data/pipeline.py

Function

Give the boosting classifier the information that separates application-layer attacks, which the 91 original features do not contain. Used by the whole pipeline (data.extended_features, implemented in fl_ids/data/features.py) and by Phase B, which derives the same features from the live capture.

How it works

  • Service port (1): the server side of each connection (smallest port below 32768), or −1 for ICMP/ARP.
  • Content (18): decoded payload length, entropy, printable share, HTTP request flag, status code, header and X- header counts, URI length/parameters/%-encoding, file-data length, special characters, and counts of six generic signature groups.
  • Flow (20): packets grouped into connections in capture order (new connection after 60 s idle or 120 s active); each packet gets its connection's packet count, duration, rate, flags, payload, request and keyword totals.
  • Never used as features: IP addresses, timestamps, ephemeral ports, row order (each identifies the capture file, i.e. the label). Exact duplicates are then dropped on the new feature set.
  • Trained with square-root inverse-frequency class weights; evaluated on a flow-grouped split so no connection is in both train and test.

3Split: server calibration set and federated pooldata/pipeline.py

Function

Carve out a small, labelled, server-held set to train the boosting model, and keep the rest for federated clients.

How it works

5% of all rows (stratified by class) become the calibration set; 20% of that is held back to score each boosting version, leaving 79,430 rows to train on [76,388 with the original features]. The remainder is stratified-subsampled to 60,000 rows for the federated pool, to keep ~150 evaluation runs affordable; after each client's test and threshold slices, 45,474 rows are left for training. So the federated system trains on about 125,000 rows; only the standalone classifier above was trained on 1.43 million. Labels live only on the server — clients never reveal local attack labels.

4Non-IID partition and per-client normalisationdata/pipeline.py

Function

Simulate realistic clients whose traffic differs wildly from each other, and prepare each client's private train/validation/test data.

How it works

  • For each class, draw per-client proportions from a Dirichlet(α) distribution — small α gives strongly skewed clients (one may be 94% benign).
  • Each client splits its rows: 15% test, 15% of the benign remainder as a benign validation slice (to calibrate its anomaly threshold), the rest train.
  • Each client z-scores its features with a scaler fit only on its own training rows (never globally — a global scaler leaks cross-client statistics), clipped to ±10 so a counter-like feature with tiny local variance can't swamp the reconstruction error.
  • A raw (un-normalised) copy is kept for the boosting model, which must always see raw features: a per-client scaler can shift a feature by orders of magnitude and break shared tree thresholds.

5Boosting bootstrap (cold start)models/boosting.py

Function

The first-pass supervised classifier: names known attack types cheaply and decisively. Trained before round 1 so clients never filter with an untrained model.

How it works

LightGBM multiclass on raw features; each boosting iteration adds one tree per class (15). The model is serialised to text so it can be broadcast to clients rather than weight-averaged. Rows are weighted by √(largest class ÷ their class) so rare classes count more, in the bootstrap and in every update. Held-out accuracy on the calibration hold-out: 0.997 (0.963 with the original features).

6Client round: boosting filter, then autoencoder trainingfl/client.py · models/autoencoder.py

Function

Each client trains an unsupervised autoencoder on its own “normal-looking” traffic, so it can later flag traffic unlike anything benign — the zero-day backstop.

How it works

  • The client runs the broadcast boosting model on its local rows and discards the ones boosting confidently calls an attack (probability ≥ 0.7). The autoencoder trains only on what is left, so it never learns attacks as “normal.”
  • Dense autoencoder 130 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 130 (9,834 parameters), MSE reconstruction loss.
  • The client returns updated weights, a 50-bin histogram of reconstruction errors, and (every 3rd round) up to 50 flagged rows with analyst-confirmed labels. Raw traffic otherwise never leaves the client.
  • Anomaly threshold = 97th percentile of reconstruction error on the client's benign validation slice, recomputed every round — so about 3% of benign traffic is flagged by construction.

7Server aggregation: trust filter + trimmed meanrobustness/ · fl/strategy.py

Function

Combine client updates into a new global autoencoder while screening out poisoned (Byzantine) updates.

How it works

  • Work on deltas (new − old weights), never raw weights — raw weights are dominated by the shared global model, so every pair looks ~0.99 similar.
  • Reference direction = coordinate-wise median of the round's deltas; per client compute cosine similarity to it and the L2 norm.
  • Exclude a client if (a) its similarity is a per-round MAD outlier, (b) its update points away from the consensus (cosine < 0), or (c) its running trust score has fallen below 0.5.
  • Norm-clip survivors to 2× the median norm, aggregate with a trimmed mean, and add the result to the round's starting weights. If nobody survives, the round is skipped.
ParameterValueEffect
robustness.mad_outlier_threshold3.0relative, per-round outlier band

trust_ema_alpha0.330% new round, 70% history
norm_clip_multiplier / trim_fraction2.0 / 0.15bound large updates; trim 15% per side

Known limits: at 50% attackers the median becomes the attackers' direction and the filter inverts; late-round attackers look like honest noise.

8Incremental boosting updatemodels/boosting_update.py

Function

Let the supervised model learn from what the autoencoders catch, without clients sharing raw traffic.

How it works

Every 3 rounds, clients that survived the trust filter surface up to 50 autoencoder-flagged rows with the label an analyst would confirm (false alarms come back as “Normal”). The server continues LightGBM training for 50 more trees on the calibration set plus the accumulated alerts. If an alert contains a class the model has never seen, it retrains from scratch — continued training can't learn a new class, because that class's softmax hessian is ~0 and the leaf-regularisation setting blocks every split. The new model is broadcast with the next round.

10Replay and feature extraction (Phase B)sdn/topology.py · sdn/feature_extraction.py

Function

Turn a real captured attack into the same 91-feature rows the models were trained on.

How it works

Mininet builds 10 hosts on one Open vSwitch switch pointed at an os-ken OpenFlow 1.3 controller; host h(i+1) is FL client i. tcpreplay replays the real .pcap from a host while tshark extracts all 61 dataset fields per packet, mapped to the authors' spelling (tshark 4.x prints booleans as True/False and shortens hex, which silently zeroed tcp.flags.ack before it was fixed). The service-port, content and flow features are derived with the same code as in training (99.8% of live rows from the Backdoor capture match its training rows exactly). Rows are one-hot encoded with the training vocabulary and scored with that client's scaler and threshold. Mininet needs root, granted by one narrow sudoers rule for the argument-less topology script.

11Verdict, mitigation bridge and flow rulesdn/live_inference.py · sdn/bridge.py · sdn/controller.py

Function

Reduce thousands of per-packet cascade outputs to one decision per device and turn it into a real flow-table change.

How it works

The device verdict is the most frequent non-benign label across its packets (mean confidence of those packets, and the cascade stage that made most of the calls), or “benign” if none. It is posted to a small REST bridge, which asks the controller to install a rule: a high-priority drop on the source IP, or an OpenFlow meter for rate-limiting. The result is verified against the switch's own ovs-ofctl dump-flows, not the bridge's say-so.

12Evaluation harness and live dashboardeval/ · dashboard/

Function

Measure what each part contributes, and watch a run as it happens.

How it works

  • Variants (same data, same seed): full pipeline, plain FedAvg, trimmed-mean-only (no cosine filter), autoencoder-only, boosting-only.
  • Headline metrics are not accuracy — ~71% of traffic is benign, so accuracy rises when a broken autoencoder stops flagging anything. Use attack macro-recall, benign FPR, detection F1, zero-day macro-recall.
  • Poisoning sweep: 0/20/30/40/50% sign-flip attackers. Zero-day: each of 14 attack classes is removed from every training set (and scaler) then scored as unseen traffic.
  • The evaluation scripts run clients in-process (not over gRPC) so dozens of runs are practical; the real multi-process path is covered by Phase A.

Caveats and limitations

Every number above is real, but every result has a scope. Full reasoning for each is in PAPER_NOTES.md.

  1. Mitigation is not real-time. Phase B classifies a full capture, not a packet stream.
  2. The DL comparison used a non-default, rebalanced sample. Its benign false-positive rates (16% boosting, 53% DL) aren't comparable to the pipeline's natural-distribution numbers (0.02% boosting FPR): the sample cut the benign class from 71% to ~10%. The relative result is sound regardless.
  3. Macro-F1 figures from different experiments are not interchangeable. Boosting scores 0.887 on the balanced 500k comparison, 0.910 on the federated run's small natural-distribution test slices (where MITM and Fingerprinting have 1 and 3 test rows), and 0.983 for the final classifier on all rows with the new features. Compare numbers only within one experiment.
  4. Random-split optimism — now checked. The final classifier is reported on a flow-grouped split (no connection in both train and test) as well as a random split; they agree within ~0.005 macro F1. Splits by whole capture file or by time period were not run.
  5. The service port is partly a testbed effect. In Edge-IIoTset benign traffic is MQTT (1883) and VNC (5900) and never HTTP, so “port 80” alone hints at an attack. In a network with benign web traffic this feature would separate much less.
  6. Flow features describe a whole connection record (up to 120 s), the same way CICFlowMeter-style datasets do. The classifier labels connection records; it is not a first-packet decision.
  7. Trade-offs of the new features. On known attacks boosting alone now has a higher detection F1 than the full cascade (0.998 vs. 0.961), because the autoencoder's ~3% by-design false alarms cost more than it adds there. Two held-out classes got worse as zero-day attacks (MITM 0.89→0.69; Uploading for boosting alone), and attacker survival at 40% rose (24%→36%, single seed).
  8. MITM cannot be scored on the flow-grouped split — its 1,229 rows are one ARP connection. On the random split its F1 is ….
  9. The remaining improvement ideas (3–6) were not run because the target was met without them; no claim is made about what they would add.
  10. Non-IID vs. malicious data is a real confound. The trust filter targets corrupted updates; an honest client with a very skewed shard can look like a poisoned one and is sometimes excluded for a round.
  11. The trust filter has a documented breaking point. At 50% malicious clients its median reference becomes the attackers' direction. Trimmed-mean alone also fails once colluding attackers exceed its trim fraction.
  12. Only one attack model tested — sign-flip with an inflated example count. Adaptive attackers are untested.
  13. Mostly single-seed federated results. A multi-seed check on one flagged anomaly (20% vs. 30% in the sweep) showed it does not hold across seeds — noise, not a trend. The final classifier was run on three seeds; most federated numbers are still single-seed.
  14. The federated system uses a sample of the data. The classifier that meets the accuracy target was trained on 1.43M rows; the federated system trains on about 125k (a 79,430-row calibration set and a 60,000-row client pool), to keep the ~150 evaluation runs affordable. Trained on only the 5% share, the classifier still reaches 99.7% / 0.979 on the same test set, but the autoencoder has not been tested at full scale.
  15. Tiny classes. MITM (358–406 rows) and Fingerprinting (853 rows) rest on few test rows.
  16. Novelty is not a settled claim. A few targeted searches found no published system combining all five parts; that is not a systematic review.
