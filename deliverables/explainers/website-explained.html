The FL-IDS website, explained simply

A walk through every section of aronnnn1.github.io/FL-IDS and its Cumulative results page: what each line says, and what it means in plain words.

How to use this: each part shows what the site says (grey), then a simple explanation (yellow), and sometimes why it matters (green) or a warning (pink). A dictionary of every attack type and every score is at the end.

The story we use throughout

Think of a big school with many classrooms. Children pass little notes to each other all day; these are the packets travelling on a network. Most notes are friendly (normal traffic), but some come from bullies (attacks). Our system is a team of helpers: guard 1 has a photo book of 14 known kinds of bully (LightGBM classifier); guard 2 knows what normal notes look like and spots anything weird (autoencoder); the classrooms learn together by sharing lessons, not notes (federated learning); a fair principal throws out lessons from sneaky classrooms (trust filter); and an automatic door locks bullies out (SDN).

1. The top bar and the title

Menu links: Summary · Boosting vs. DL · Confusion matrices · Training time · Accuracy ≥ 95% · Pipeline · Step-by-step · Verification · Caveats · Cumulative results → and a Light/Dark mode button.
This is the table of contents. Clicking a word jumps to that part of the page. The button changes the colours from white to dark, like turning the lights down. “Cumulative results” opens a second page with every table of numbers in one place.
Title: “Boosting vs. Deep Learning, and the full FL-IDS pipeline”.
The page tells two stories: a contest between two kinds of guard 1 (a “boosting” guard and a “deep-learning” guard), and the whole system from start to finish (the “pipeline”, like a factory line).
“A federated, cascade-based intrusion detection system for IoT/IIoT traffic: a supervised LightGBM classifier as the first pass, with a client-side autoencoder as a federated, poisoning-resistant backstop for zero-day attacks.”
Word by word: intrusion detection system = a burglar alarm for networks. IoT/IIoT traffic = notes passed between smart gadgets in homes and factories. federated = many classrooms learn together without handing over their notes. cascade = a line of checks, one after another, like two guards at two gates. supervised LightGBM classifier as the first pass = guard 1, who learned from labelled examples (“this note is from bully X”). client-side autoencoder = guard 2, who lives in each classroom. poisoning-resistant = hard to trick during training. backstop for zero-day attacks = a safety net for bullies nobody has ever seen.
“This site reports (1) a head-to-head comparison of boosting against a deep-learning classifier, (2) how long things take and why, (3) how the classifier was pushed past 0.95 macro F1 and 95% accuracy — and checked on a stricter, connection-held-out test, (4) a visual and step-by-step explanation of the whole pipeline, and (5) verification that it works end to end, with its limitations stated plainly.”
The five things you'll find: (1) which kind of guard 1 is better; (2) how long the computer took for each job; (3) how we got the score above our 95% goal, and proved we didn't cheat by testing on whole conversations the system had never seen; (4) pictures and steps showing how everything works; (5) proof that it all works together, plus what it still can't do.
“Dataset: Edge-IIoTset (Ferrag et al., 2022). All numbers come from real runs of the code in this repository. Full notes and sources: PAPER_NOTES.md.”
The notes we learned from come from a public collection called Edge-IIoTset, recorded by researchers (Ferrag and team) on a real test network with more than ten kinds of sensors. Every number on the site came from actually running our programs; nothing was made up. PAPER_NOTES.md is our diary of every experiment.
“Project status: complete (16 October 2026). Every phase is implemented, run on the real data and verified end to end; the research paper is being written.”
The project is finished: every part was built, tried on the real notes and checked from start to end. The only thing left is writing it up as a research paper.

3. Boosting vs. a supervised deep-learning classifier

“If you swap LightGBM boosting for a deep-learning classifier as the cascade's first pass — same attack-type labels, same task, comparable effort — which is more accurate? Both were trained and evaluated on the exact same 500,000-row sample (400k train / 100k test, stratified); boosting on raw features, the DL classifier on standardized features.”
A fair race between two guards. Boosting (LightGBM) is a team of hundreds of tiny yes/no questions (“Is the note longer than 100 letters? Is it sent to door 80?”) whose votes are added up. Deep learning (a small neural network, an “MLP” with 8,271 adjustable knobs) learns by mixing all the numbers together through layers. Both got the same 500,000 notes: 400,000 to study and 100,000 for the exam. “Stratified” means each bully type appears in study and exam in the same proportions. “Standardized” means the deep-learning guard got the numbers rescaled to similar sizes, because that's what it needs; the boosting guard doesn't need that.

“Per-class recall: where the gap actually is”

Bars for each of the 15 classes, Boost vs DL, e.g. XSS 0.761 vs 0.072, SQL_injection 0.767 vs 0.183, Fingerprinting 0.579 vs 0.000, Port_Scanning 0.997 vs 0.498 … DDoS_TCP/UDP 1.000 vs 1.000, Password 0.831 vs 0.860.
Recall = “of all the bullies of this type, how many did you catch?” Each pair of bars is one bully type. Boosting catches 76% of XSS bullies; deep learning catches only 7%. For floods (DDoS) both catch everything, because floods are loud and obvious. The bars are sorted so the biggest differences come first. Password is the one type where DL is slightly better (0.860 vs 0.831), but only because DL guesses “Password” for almost everything (see the confusion matrices).
“Why boosting wins here.” Trees split directly on the numbers (packet counts, flags, protocol fields); a small network must learn the same boundaries through weighted sums, and with 400k rows and 15 epochs it doesn't manage it for subtle classes.
Our data is a big spreadsheet of numbers. Boosting asks sharp questions like “is this number above 5?”, which is exactly how spreadsheet data separates. The small network has to blend everything and learn slowly; with 15 practice passes (“epochs”) it never learned the fine differences between sneaky web attacks and normal traffic.
“Honest caveat.” The DL model was deliberately small; a bigger one might do better. The right claim is “this DL classifier doesn't beat boosting here”, not “DL cannot work”.
We only raced against a small deep-learning guard. A bigger one, trained longer, might do better, and we say so instead of overclaiming.

4. Confusion matrices

“Rows are the true class, columns the predicted class. Cell colour is the fraction of that true class landing in that column, so the outlined diagonal reads as recall. Hover a cell for the exact count.” Buttons: Counts / % of true class.
A confusion matrix is a scoreboard of mix-ups. Each row is what the note really was; each column is what the guard said it was. The diagonal (top-left to bottom-right) is where the guard was right. Anything off the diagonal is a mistake: row “Normal”, column “Password” = friendly notes the guard wrongly called password attacks. Darker colour = more notes. “Counts” shows how many notes; “% of true class” shows what share of that row. The colour bar shows 0% to 100% of the row.

Boosting's matrix (500k comparison, original clues)

Mostly a strong diagonal, with some mix-ups among web traffic. Examples you can read off the table: of 9,848 real Normal notes, 8,273 were called Normal, but 716 were called Password, 521 SQL_injection and 325 DDoS_HTTP. Of 7,361 Uploading notes, only 4,634 were right; 1,443 were called DDoS_HTTP and 553 Port_Scanning. Floods (DDoS_TCP, DDoS_UDP: 9,848 each) are perfect.
“Boosting's errors sit mostly inside one application-layer cluster — DDoS_HTTP, Password, SQL_injection, Uploading, XSS and Normal — which together account for 84% (9,225 of 10,921) of all its mistakes.”
Of all 10,921 mistakes, 84% were confusions within one family of six web-related types. These all look alike because they all talk to the same web server. That told us exactly where to improve.

Deep learning's matrix

“Password absorbs large fractions of DDoS_HTTP, Normal, SQL_injection, Uploading and Vulnerability_scanner — a strong ‘when unsure, guess Password’ bias … The same cluster holds 78% (25,224 of 32,207) of its mistakes, but it also fails on Port_Scanning ↔ DDoS_TCP and Fingerprinting.”
The deep-learning guard made three times as many mistakes (32,207), and had a bad habit: when unsure, it shouted “Password!”. Of 9,848 Normal notes it called 4,532 Password, and of 3,013 XSS notes it called 1,981 Password. It also called half of the Port_Scanning notes (1,990 of 3,995) DDoS_TCP, and never once got Fingerprinting right.

Final classifier's matrix (after our improvements)

Boosting with the new clues and class weights, on all 1,985,768 rows: trained on 1,429,825 and tested on 397,073 from whole connections never seen in training. “The HTTP-family cluster holds 67% (744 of 1,112 remaining errors).” Remaining mix-ups: Password ↔ DDoS_HTTP, Ransomware → Backdoor. “MITM has no row here … on the random split its F1 is 0.994.”
This exam was much bigger (about 397,000 notes), and the guard made only 1,112 mistakes in total. Examples: of 287,319 Normal notes, 287,082 were right; of 10,197 SQL_injection notes, 10,191 were right. Remaining mix-ups: 406 Password notes called DDoS_HTTP and 198 the other way (both knock on the same website), and 98 Ransomware notes called Backdoor (both use door number 4321). MITM is missing because all of its notes are one single conversation, which can't be split between studying and the exam. On the normal (random) exam MITM scores 0.994.

5. Training time: how long, and why

“Training either classifier is quick (boosting ~1 minute, DL ~10 seconds). What is slow is the evaluation methodology — the poisoning sweep and zero-day experiments retrain the whole federated pipeline 75 and 14 times.”
Teaching one guard is fast. What takes hours is our testing: to prove things, we rebuild the whole school's training from scratch again and again (75 times for one test, 14 for another), like rehearsing a whole play 75 times.
Bar: Boosting 55.5 s, “5.4× slower”; DL classifier 10.2 s, “baseline”.

“VM time = time on the Linux VM's own clock; the VM pauses whenever the Mac sleeps … single measurements, not averaged.”
Our programs ran inside a pretend computer (a virtual machine) on a MacBook. When the Mac slept, the pretend computer's clock paused too, so these times are “awake time”. Each was measured once.

“Why a full federated run takes ~4 minutes when a round takes seconds”

  • Boosting updates are the bottleneck. Every 3 rounds guard 1 gets extra lessons. That round takes about 43 s instead of 5–7 s: classrooms spend about 10 s checking notes with the bigger guard, and the principal spends about 33 s teaching it 50 more trees per type. Six of these per run take about three-quarters of the time (estimated from timestamps).
  • Single-threaded on purpose. If guard 1 studies with several cores at once, tiny timing differences change the result. One bully type's score jumped between 0.0 and 0.88 on identical runs! So we force one core: slower, but repeatable.
  • The model grows. Each update adds trees (300 → 600), so later rounds are slower.
  • Evaluation multiplies everything. Every test point is a full retraining, so minutes become hours.
  • Modest hardware. No graphics card (not needed: our brains are tiny), 4 cores, limited memory: 11 programs loading big libraries nearly ran out until we limited their helper threads.
  • Data loading every time. Reading the 1.16 GB file takes 15–25 s per program start.
“How to speed it up (none applied yet)”: cache the cleaned data, update guard 1 less often, allow multi-core for exploring, use a machine with more memory.
Ideas to make it faster that we haven't done yet: save the cleaned notes so we don't re-read them, give guard 1 fewer extra lessons, use several cores when we're just exploring, and get a computer with more memory.

6. How macro F1 was pushed past 0.95

“The target was ≥ 95% accuracy and ≥ 0.95 macro F1. With the original 91 features, boosting reached 0.887 macro F1 on the 500k comparison sample, and 0.889 macro F1 / 96.4% accuracy on all 1.9M rows. Ideas 1, 2, 7 and 8 below were implemented and measured … 0.983 macro F1 and 99.7% accuracy … Ideas 3–6 turned out not to be needed.”
Our goal: at least 95 right out of 100, and a fairness score (macro F1) of at least 0.95. With the original 91 clues per note we had 96.4% but only 0.889 fairness, because some bully types were badly caught. We tried four ideas; they got us to 99.7% and 0.983. Four other ideas were never needed.

“Before: the F1 budget of the original features”

“Macro F1 is the plain average of 15 per-class F1 scores, so reaching 0.95 needed the per-class F1 sum to rise from 13.31 (= 0.887 × 15) to 14.25 — a gain of 0.94 F1 points.” Bars: Fingerprinting F1 0.683 (−0.267), Uploading 0.761, DDoS_HTTP 0.762, SQL_injection 0.768, Password 0.787, XSS 0.857, Normal 0.905, Port_Scanning 0.916, Ransomware 0.933, then Backdoor 0.962 and the rest “ok”.
Imagine 15 school subjects with marks out of 1. The average must reach 0.95, so all marks together must add up to 14.25. We had 13.31, so we were 0.94 marks short. The red bit of each bar shows how far each subject was below 0.95. The weakest “subjects” were Fingerprinting, Uploading, DDoS_HTTP, SQL_injection and Password.

“Why the original features were stuck”

“In SQL_injection, XSS, Uploading and Password traffic only about 5–17% of packets carry any HTTP content. The rest are bare TCP handshake/acknowledgement packets to port 80 … 2.31% of rows share an identical 91-feature vector with a row of a different class, which caps macro recall at 0.969 for any per-packet model.”
Most notes in web attacks are empty envelopes saying “got it” (TCP acknowledgements) sent to the website door (port 80). Only 5–17 of every 100 actually have a message inside. Empty envelopes from different bullies look exactly the same. We counted: 2.31% of all notes had an identical twin with a different answer. When twins have different answers, no guard can get both right, so even a perfect guard could reach at most 0.969. The clues that tell them apart were in columns the dataset's standard recipe throws away.

The eight ideas, one by one

IdeaStatusWhat it means
1. Behaviour featuresdoneGroup notes into conversations (same two devices, same doors; a new conversation after 60 s of silence or after 120 s in total). Every note gets 20 conversation clues: how many notes, how long, how fast, how many “hello/goodbye/hang-up” signals (SYN/FIN/RST), total content, web requests, bad-word totals. Now empty envelopes carry their conversation's story. Biggest jump: 0.923 → 0.981. IP addresses and times are used only to group, never as clues.
2. Content as numbersdone18 number clues about the message: length, how jumbled it is (entropy), share of readable letters, whether it's a web request, the web reply code, header counts, web-address length and parts, special characters, and counts of six groups of tell-tale words (database tricks, script tricks, uploads, logins, sneaky folder paths, names of hacking tools). Plus service port: which door the conversation uses (80 = website, 1883 = sensor messages, 4321 = what the backdoor uses). The random “return-address” doors are not used, because their numbers follow the recording time, which would give away the answer. The port alone: 0.889 → 0.924.
3. Per-class thresholdsnot neededA trick to tune when to say each answer. The problem it fixed (e.g. Uploading being under-called) disappeared: Uploading is now about 1.00/1.00.
4. Specialist modelnot neededA second guard just for the web family. That family, which held 84% of the old mistakes, is now almost clean; the leftover Password/DDoS_HTTP mix-up is only 2–4%.
5. Tuning settingspartlyWe set the guard's study settings sensibly (63 leaves per tree, learning speed 0.1, uses 80% of clues and rows each round, stops when it stops improving for 50 rounds), but didn't search for the best settings. The new clues did the work.
6. Other boostersnot neededOther tree libraries or mixing several. Not needed: the guard is already near the best possible score for these clues (the “ceiling”, 0.9999).
7. Class weightsdoneRare bully types count a bit more (square-root rule). Without it, the score wobbled between tries (0.981, 0.956, 0.952) because Fingerprinting, with only 156 conversations, scored anywhere from 0.49 to 0.88. With it: steady at 0.983–0.985, Fingerprinting about 0.93. Even studying only 5% of the data: 99.7% and 0.979.
8. Check it's realdoneWe used the strict conversation-level exam and three seeds. This caught a real problem: all normal “remote screen” (VNC) traffic was one conversation of 264,094 notes. It landed entirely in the exam, so the guard had never seen anything like it and called 16% of normal notes bullies. Cutting long conversations into 2-minute pieces, which is what standard network tools do, fixed it. The old clues scored the same on both exams, so they weren't cheating either.

“Assumptions behind the final number”

  • Per connection record, up to 120 s: the guard judges a whole conversation piece, not the very first note.
  • The service port is partly a testbed effect: in this dataset friendly traffic never used the website door, so “door 80” already hints “bully”. In a real office with normal web browsing it would help less.
  • Natural mix: 71% friendly notes, all notes after removing duplicates, 3 seeds.
  • Now used everywhere: the whole-school training, every experiment and the live demo all use the 130 clues. Numbers in [brackets] on the site are the old 91-clue results.

7. The pipeline at a glance

“Two phases, kept deliberately separate: Phase A trains the models offline (federated), and Phase B loads the saved bundle and enforces decisions on live traffic.” Legend colours: Data, Boosting (supervised), Federated clients/autoencoder, Server-side robustness, Network/SDN, Output artefact.
Phase A = school term: everyone studies, then the trained guards are packed into a box (the “bundle”). Phase B = the guards go on duty at the door. Keeping them apart means study problems and door problems can't get mixed up. The colours on the diagram say which kind of job each box is.

“The cascade decision rule (one implementation, used by evaluation, dashboard and SDN)”

  1. Guard 1 guesses a type and how sure it is.
  2. If it's an attack and it's at least 70% sure, that's the final answer; guard 2 isn't asked.
  3. Otherwise guard 2 measures how badly it can rebuild the note (the “reconstruction error”). If that's above this classroom's alarm level (“threshold”), the answer is “anomalous” (maybe a brand-new bully), with confidence = how far above the line it is, capped at 1. Otherwise it's “benign”.
The exact same rule is used in testing, on the dashboard and at the door, so the scores we report are the same decisions the door makes.

8. “Every step explained” (the 12 fold-out steps)

“What each stage does, how it works, and the parameters that control it. Values are the ones used in this project's runs (configs/config.yaml); module paths are relative to fl_ids/.”
Each step opens like a drawer. Inside: Function (what it's for), How it works, and Parameters (the settings knobs, all kept in one settings file, config.yaml). The grey text on the right of each step is the program file that does it.

Step 1: Load, clean and encode the data

  • Function: turn the dataset file into a clean number table X (1,909,719 × 91) and answers y (15 types as numbers).
  • Read 47 of the 63 columns: drop 15 name-tag/time/raw-message columns as the dataset's authors suggest, plus Attack_label, which is just “attack or not” and would hand the guard the answer.
  • Fix the placeholder: “doesn't apply” was written 0 in some recordings and 0.0 in others, and the spelling matched the answer. One column alone (mqtt.topic_0.0) told friendly from bully perfectly (AUROC 1.0 = perfect separation). We made both spellings the same; now the best single clue only reaches about 0.71. We spotted this ourselves, but Galal (arXiv, August 2026) published it first, so the site credits that paper.
  • Drop empty rows and 309,497 exact copies.
  • Repair MITM: the MITM rows had values under the wrong column names. We replaced the 1,214 broken rows with 1,229 rows rebuilt from the original recording. This one is our own find.
  • Turn 7 word columns (like the web method “GET”) into yes/no number columns → 91 clues. Parameters list the counts and the 7 columns; if the repaired MITM file is missing, the program stops loudly instead of silently using broken data.

Step 2: Service-port, content and flow features

  • Function: give guard 1 the clues that separate web attacks. First tested on guard 1 alone, now plugged into the whole system: training, every experiment and the live demo. The same code makes the clues in training and live, and 99.8% of live notes came out exactly equal to their training copies.
  • Service port (1 clue), content (18 clues) and flow/conversation (20 clues), as described in section 6. Never used as clues: IP addresses, times, random return doors, row order (each would reveal which recording, and so which answer).
  • Class weights and the strict conversation-level exam.
  • Parameters: 91 → 130 clues; 1,985,768 notes after removing copies; 60 s / 120 s conversation cut-offs (the 120 s one is the CICFlowMeter tool's default); 63 leaves, learning speed 0.1, stop after 50 rounds without improvement; weight = √(biggest class size ÷ this class size).

Step 3: Split: server calibration set and federated pool

  • 5% of notes (same mix of types) go to the principal with answers; 20% of that is kept for testing each new version of guard 1, leaving 79,430 to teach with (76,388 with the old clues). In the first runs the rest was trimmed to 60,000 notes for classrooms (45,474 left to learn from, about 125,000 in all). In the newest runs the classrooms get all the rest, about 1.89 million notes, with each conversation kept together. Only the stand-alone guard 1 in section 6 studied 1.43 million. Answers stay with the principal; classrooms never reveal their notes' answers.

Step 4: Non-IID partition and per-client normalisation

  • For each note type, a random “Dirichlet” draw decides how much of it each classroom gets. Small α (0.3) = very uneven; one classroom may be 94% friendly notes.
  • Each classroom keeps 15% for its exam and 15% of its friendly notes to set its alarm level; the rest is for study.
  • Each classroom rescales numbers using only its own study notes (a z-score: how many “typical steps” from average), capped at ±10 so one odd counter can't drown out everything.
  • Guard 1 always gets the raw, un-rescaled numbers: each classroom's rescaling is different and would confuse a shared guard.
  • Note: classroom 5 got no friendly practice notes, so its alarm never rings. We report this rather than hide it.

Step 5: Boosting bootstrap (cold start)

  • Guard 1 studies before round 1, on raw numbers, adding one tree per type per round (15 trees per round). Settings: 300 rounds, learning speed 0.05, 31 leaves. Leaf regularisation (min_sum_hessian, lambda_l2) keeps it calm; without it, training went wild and false alarms jumped between 0.1% and 16% when just 1% of the notes changed. “Deterministic, one thread” = same answer every time. Rare types get a bit more weight (√ class weights). Practice-test accuracy: 0.997 (was 0.963).

Step 6: Client round: boosting filter, then autoencoder training

  • Each classroom removes notes guard 1 confidently calls bullies, then trains guard 2 only on the rest, so guard 2 never learns bullies as “normal”.
  • Guard 2's shape: 130 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 130 (9,834 knobs; 7,299 with the old 91 clues). It squeezes and rebuilds; mistakes are measured with MSE.
  • It sends back its knob changes, a 50-bar chart of its rebuilding errors and, every third round, up to 50 confirmed suspicious notes. Otherwise notes never leave.
  • Alarm level = the 97th percentile of rebuilding error on its friendly practice notes: the level that only 3% of friendly notes exceed. So about 3% false alarms, by design.
  • Settings: learning speed 0.001, 5 passes, 64 notes at a time; 97th percentile; 0.7 confidence for guard 1.

Step 7: Server aggregation: trust filter + trimmed mean

  • The principal compares changes, not whole brains: whole brains all look about 99% alike because they start from the same copy.
  • The reference is the median change (the middle value for every knob). For each classroom it measures the cosine similarity (do the changes point the same way? +1 = same, 0 = unrelated, −1 = opposite) and the size.
  • A classroom is excluded if (a) it is an outlier compared with the others this round (MAD test), (b) its change points the opposite way (cosine < 0), or (c) its long-term trust score has fallen below 0.5.
  • Remaining changes are size-capped at 2× the typical size and averaged with a trimmed mean (drop the top and bottom 15% for each knob). If nobody passes, the round is skipped.
  • Settings: MAD 3.0; minimum cosine 0; minimum trust 0.5; trust memory 0.3 (30% this round, 70% history); cap 2.0; trim 0.15.
  • Known limits: at 50% sneaky classrooms, the “middle” becomes theirs; late in training, sneaky changes can look like honest noise.

Step 8: Incremental boosting update

  • Every 3 rounds, honest classrooms send up to 50 confirmed suspicious notes (false alarms come back as “Normal”). Guard 1 adds 50 trees per type. If a note is a brand-new type, guard 1 is retrained from scratch, because adding trees can't teach a type it has never seen. The new guard 1 is sent out next round. Trade-off: those few confirmed notes do leave the classroom. This is also the slowest step.

Step 9: The Phase A bundle

  • The real run (1 principal + 10 classroom programs, 20 rounds) saves guard 1, guard 2's knobs, each classroom's rescaling and alarm level, and a manifest (settings, scores, seed, code version). Before saving, it recomputes the alarm levels and checks they match; a mismatched box is rejected. Settings: 20 rounds; principal's address 127.0.0.1:9091; option to add sneaky classrooms for demos.

Step 10: Replay and feature extraction (Phase B)

  • Mininet builds 10 computers on one Open vSwitch with the os-ken controller; computer h(i+1) is classroom i. tcpreplay plays the recording; tshark extracts all 61 facts per note, and the same code as training adds the 39 extra clues. Newer tshark writes True/False and short hex numbers, which silently zeroed one clue until we fixed it. Mininet needs administrator rights, given by one narrow permission rule.

Step 11: Verdict, mitigation bridge and flow rule

  • The device's verdict is its most common bully label (or benign). It goes to the bridge, which asks the controller for a drop rule (≥ 0.85) or a meter/speed limit (≥ 0.5). We check the switch's own rule list, not just the bridge's word. Ports: 6653 for the controller, 8080 for the bridge. Limitation: it needs the whole recording first, so it's not note-by-note.

Step 12: Evaluation harness and live dashboard

  • Variants compared fairly: full system; plain averaging (FedAvg, no trust filter); trimmed mean only; guard 2 only; guard 1 only.
  • Headline scores are not plain accuracy, because 71% of notes are friendly, so a broken guard that never shouts still looks “accurate”.
  • Poisoning sweep at 0/20/30/40/50% sneaky classrooms; zero-day test hiding each of 14 types.
  • Tests run all classrooms inside one program to save time; the real multi-program version is Phase A.
  • The Streamlit dashboard is a live web page showing the training and the door as they happen.

10. Caveats and limitations (all 16)

“Every number above is real, but every result has a scope.”
  1. Mitigation is not real-time: the door locks after the recording is analysed.
  2. The DL contest used a rebalanced sample: friendly notes were cut from 71% to 10%, so its false-alarm numbers (16%, 53%) can't be compared with the system's normal ones (0.02%). Who won is still fair.
  3. Different experiments' F1 can't be compared: 0.887 (contest), 0.910 (small federated test, where MITM had 1 test note and Fingerprinting 3), 0.983 (final). Compare only within one experiment.
  4. Random-split optimism, now checked: the strict and random exams agree within about 0.005. Splits by whole recording file or by time weren't tried.
  5. Service port is partly a testbed effect (no friendly website traffic in this dataset).
  6. Flow clues describe a whole conversation piece (≤ 120 s): not a first-note decision.
  7. Trade-offs of the new clues: on bullies it already knows, guard 1 alone now scores better than both guards together (0.998 vs 0.961) because of guard 2's planned 3% false alarms; two hidden bully types got harder to catch (MITM 0.89 → 0.69, and Uploading for guard 1 alone); and at 40% sneaky classrooms more sneaky changes got through (24% → 36%, one seed only).
  8. MITM can't be scored on the strict exam: it's one conversation (1,229 notes). On the random exam its F1 is 0.994.
  9. Ideas 3–6 weren't run: no claims about them.
  10. Unusual but honest classrooms can look sneaky: a classroom with very unusual notes sometimes gets excluded for a round.
  11. The filter breaks at 50%; trimmed mean alone also breaks once sneaky classrooms outnumber what it trims.
  12. Two kinds of sneaky classroom tested (sign-flip, and a clever one that knows the filter and mostly slips past it); other clever ones are untested.
  13. Whole-school tests now use three seeds for the main experiments (ablation, sweep, new-bully test); the pruning and clever-attacker tests use one. One odd pattern (20% vs 30%) disappeared with other seeds, so it was noise.
  14. The whole-school system now uses all the notes (about 1.89 million shared out, conversations kept together; see the Cumulative page, 7d), but guard 1 inside it still learns from the principal's 5% (about 72–80 thousand notes). The first 150 or so test runs used a 60,000-note sample, and those results are kept for comparison.
  15. Tiny classes: MITM (358–406 notes) and Fingerprinting (853) give few exam notes, so their scores wobble.
  16. Novelty isn't proven: our searches found no system with all five parts, but that's not a complete review.
